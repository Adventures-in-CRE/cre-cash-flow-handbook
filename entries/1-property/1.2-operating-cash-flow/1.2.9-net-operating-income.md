## Purpose

Net operating income is what the property earns after operating expenses and
before debt, capital costs, and income tax. A buyer capitalizes it into value
and a lender sizes debt against it, so an argument about a deal is usually an
argument about this line.

## Inputs

Effective Gross Income (1.2.7). Operating Expenses (1.2.8).

## Output

One figure per period, in the model's currency, on the model's period grid.

## Method

Subtract operating expenses from effective gross income.

The work is in the boundary rather than the arithmetic. Capital reserves,
tenant improvements, leasing commissions, debt service, and depreciation all
sit below this line. Keeping them out is what lets one property's NOI be
compared against another's, and it is why a cap rate applied to a loosely
defined NOI produces a value nobody can defend.

## Rules

1. `NOI = Effective Gross Income (1.2.7) - Operating Expenses (1.2.8)`
2. Computed once per period, on the model's period grid.
3. Excludes capital expenditure reserves (1.2.10), tenant improvements and
   leasing commissions (1.2.11), debt service (1.2.13), income tax,
   depreciation, and amortization.
4. Property type changes which accounts roll up into Operating Expenses
   (1.2.8). It does not change this formula.
5. A management fee computed as a percentage of effective gross income is an
   operating expense and reduces NOI.

## Worked example

A single period on an annual grid.

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Effective Gross Income (1.2.7) | 1,000,000 |
| Operating Expenses (1.2.8) | (380,000) |
| **Net Operating Income** | **620,000** |

Capital reserves of 25,000 and leasing commissions of 40,000 fall in the same
period. Neither appears above. NOI is 620,000, not 555,000.
